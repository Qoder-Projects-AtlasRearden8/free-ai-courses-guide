# The 90-day plain-English AI plan

> A humane learning plan built around short sessions, small artifacts, and real life.

[Guide home](README.md) · [Learning paths](learning-paths.md) · [Certificate guide](certificates.md) · [Full library](full-library.md)

This plan assumes you have a job, a family, or a questionable relationship with your calendar. Three focused sessions a week are enough. Every week has a small finish line, every phase has a checkpoint, and two flex days are built into each 30-day stretch. You do not need to finish every resource—the plan is the spine, and the library is the shelf you choose from.

**Standard pace:** Standard pace is about 4–5 hours per week. On a hard week, do the first task only. On a good week, add the stretch. Progress is not a personality test.

## House rules

- The plan is the spine; the library is the shelf. You do not need to finish every course.
- Three short sessions a week count. Missing a week is data, not a moral failure.
- Always protect privacy first: no patient information, client records, passwords, API keys, or private financial data in prompts or repositories.
- Use AI for drafts, questions, and scaffolding. Keep human judgment for decisions, claims, and anything high-stakes.
- When a resource stops helping, stop. Provider loyalty is not a learning outcome.
- If you are exhausted, do the ten-minute version. Rest is part of the schedule.

## Days 1–30: See clearly

Get the vocabulary, practice a real prompt, and build a habit of checking the output. The goal is calm competence, not speed.

### Week 1: Make a small, safe start

**2–3 hours · Focus:** Orient, choose one safe problem, and put learning on the calendar.

- [ ] **Take Google's 45-minute Introduction to Generative AI.**
  Finish the quiz if you want the free skill badge. Save two examples that feel genuinely useful.
  Resource: [Introduction to Generative AI](https://www.skills.google/course_templates/536) (45 min; free certificate)
- [ ] **Create one learning note with a no-sensitive-data rule.**
  Name it something plain, like ai-lab.md. Add three tasks AI might help with, then circle one that uses no patient, client, financial, or password data.
- [ ] **Block three 45-minute sessions this week.**
  Calendar them. Put your notes in one place. A small repeatable slot beats a heroic weekend.

**If you have extra time:** Skim the four learning paths and choose a home base.

**Keep this:** One safe starter task, one place for notes, and three appointments with yourself.

### Week 2: Learn the words

**3–4 hours · Focus:** Replace vague AI anxiety with a handful of accurate, plain-English definitions.

- [ ] **Work through the first two chapters of Elements of AI.**
  No math needed. Write down one thing AI cannot do well.
  Resource: [Elements of AI: Introduction to AI](https://www.elementsofai.com/) (~30 hours)
- [ ] **Build a six-term glossary in your own words.**
  Define model, prompt, token, context window, hallucination, and inference. If a definition needs a second jargon word, simplify it.
- [ ] **Explain one concept back in five sentences.**
  Write it or record a voice note. Notice where you reach for jargon and where the idea is genuinely yours.

**If you have extra time:** Watch one 3Blue1Brown neural-network video and sketch the idea afterward.

**Keep this:** A one-page glossary you can actually use in conversation.

### Week 3: Practice the prompt loop

**3–4 hours · Focus:** Move from 'please make this better' to instructions you can test and improve.

- [ ] **Take the free lessons in ChatGPT Prompt Engineering for Developers.**
  If you do not code yet, watch for the patterns rather than copying the API details.
  Resource: [ChatGPT Prompt Engineering for Developers](https://www.deeplearning.ai/short-courses/chatgpt-prompt-engineering-for-developers/) (1 hr 40 min)
- [ ] **Write a five-part prompt template: task, context, constraints, format, and example.**
  Test it on one real, non-sensitive task such as rewriting a public paragraph or planning a meal from ingredients you already have.
- [ ] **Change one variable at a time and compare two outputs.**
  Log which version was more useful and why. This is how prompting becomes a skill rather than a stack of magic phrases.

**If you have extra time:** Read Google's prompt-engineering whitepaper for the reasoning behind the template.

**Keep this:** A reusable prompt template and notes on why it works.

### Week 4: Make a tiny useful thing

**3–5 hours · Focus:** Turn the vocabulary and prompt practice into one edited artifact and your own safety rules.

- [ ] **Make one small artifact with AI help: a checklist, email draft, study guide, or scene.**
  Use AI for a draft. Do the final editing, fact-checking, and decisions yourself.
- [ ] **Fact-check every factual claim and add a 'how I checked' note.**
  Prefer primary sources. If you cannot verify something, label it as uncertain or remove it.
- [ ] **Complete Kaggle's Intro to AI Ethics and write five personal AI rules.**
  Cover privacy, verification, disclosure, human review, and one situation where you will not use AI at all.
  Resource: [Kaggle: Intro to AI Ethics](https://www.kaggle.com/learn/intro-to-ai-ethics) (4 hours; free certificate)

**If you have extra time:** Show the artifact to one trusted person and ask what felt confusing.

**Keep this:** A finished small artifact, a verification habit, and a personal rules card.

### Day 30 checkpoint: you can explain it

You do not need a credential yet. You need evidence that the ideas are becoming yours.

- [ ] Explain generative AI to a friend in five minutes without hiding behind jargon.
- [ ] Show one artifact that you edited and checked yourself.
- [ ] Name three types of information you will never paste into a consumer AI tool.
- [ ] Choose one next step: stay on this route or move into Python and data.

## Days 31–60: Build the mechanics

Add Python and data skills, train a first model, and practice honest evaluation. Keep the artifacts small enough to finish.

### Week 5: Python, gently

**4–5 hours · Focus:** Learn enough code to read, run, and modify a small script—not to memorize a language.

- [ ] **Complete Kaggle's Python course.**
  Type every exercise. Do not copy-paste your way through the first pass.
  Resource: [Kaggle: Python](https://www.kaggle.com/learn/python) (5 hours; free certificate)
- [ ] **Write a 20-line Python script with a variable, list, loop, and function.**
  Use your own text file or simple numbers. No personal data yet.
- [ ] **Start a bug log: error, guess, what fixed it.**
  Three lines per bug. It becomes evidence of progress when the results feel invisible.

**If you have extra time:** Try the first project in freeCodeCamp's Scientific Computing with Python curriculum.

**Keep this:** Enough Python to be curious rather than afraid.

### Week 6: Make data legible

**4–5 hours · Focus:** Learn to inspect tabular data before you try to model it.

- [ ] **Complete Kaggle's Pandas course.**
  Focus on selecting, filtering, grouping, and missing values.
  Resource: [Kaggle: Pandas](https://www.kaggle.com/learn/pandas) (4 hours; free certificate)
- [ ] **Load a public dataset, check its shape and missing values, and make two charts.**
  Use Kaggle datasets or a public open-data portal. No private business data yet.
- [ ] **Write a five-sentence data story and one thing the data does not show.**
  The second part matters as much as the first. A chart without a limitation is a sales pitch, not analysis.

**If you have extra time:** Clean one messy column and document every choice you made.

**Keep this:** A notebook and a habit of asking what the data cannot tell you.

### Week 7: Train a first model

**4–5 hours · Focus:** Build the simplest model you can and learn why the score is not the whole story.

- [ ] **Complete Kaggle's Intro to Machine Learning.**
  Train a decision tree and a random forest. Note the difference in results.
  Resource: [Kaggle: Intro to Machine Learning](https://www.kaggle.com/learn/intro-to-machine-learning) (3 hours; free certificate)
- [ ] **Work through the ML framing, regression, classification, and overfitting sections of Google's crash course.**
  You do not need every module. Prioritize the ones that explain evaluation.
  Resource: [Machine Learning Crash Course](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course) (~15 hours)
- [ ] **Explain train, validation, and test sets using a non-tech analogy.**
  If the analogy breaks, fix the analogy, not your honesty.

**If you have extra time:** Read the early chapters of Machine Learning Yearning.

**Keep this:** A first model and an explanation of why a single score is not proof.

### Week 8: Tell an honest story

**5–6 hours · Focus:** Turn a dataset into a small project with a clear question, baseline, and limitation.

- [ ] **Choose a public dataset, one question, a baseline, and a train/test split.**
  Write these down before modeling. A baseline can be as simple as predicting the average.
- [ ] **Train a simple model and record two metrics—not only accuracy.**
  Explain why each metric fits or fails the question.
- [ ] **Write a README with the question, data, method, limits, and next step. Ask one person for feedback.**
  This is the portfolio piece you can show later. Clarity beats complexity.

**If you have extra time:** Try Kaggle's Intermediate Machine Learning for pipelines and cross-validation.

**Keep this:** Project 01: a small, honest data story with a README.

### Day 60 checkpoint: you can build and evaluate

You have moved from talking about AI to making a small, testable artifact with data.

- [ ] Run a Python script from top to bottom without step-by-step hand-holding.
- [ ] Show one notebook with a question, two charts, and a stated limitation.
- [ ] Explain why you chose your model and metric.
- [ ] Ask someone to critique the project—and write down what you would change.

## Days 61–90: Make it real

Connect an LLM to code, understand agent loops and MCP, then ship one small, documented tool with safety boundaries.

### Week 9: Call an LLM from code

**4–6 hours · Focus:** Move from chat windows to a small program with inputs, outputs, and secrets handled properly.

- [ ] **Work through the first half of AI Python for Beginners.**
  The API examples are optional; the point is code confidence. Use a free-tier or local model if you prefer.
  Resource: [AI Python for Beginners](https://www.deeplearning.ai/short-courses/ai-python-for-beginners/) (11.5 hours)
- [ ] **Build a tiny CLI that takes text and returns a labeled summary.**
  Put API keys in environment variables. Never paste a key into a tracked file, screenshot, or chat.
- [ ] **Add a README with setup, failure cases, and a privacy note.**
  Document what happens when the input is empty, the API fails, or the output is nonsense.

**If you have extra time:** Try a local model with a small open model runner and compare latency, quality, and privacy.

**Keep this:** A small AI script you can run, explain, and safely share.

### Week 10: Build an agent loop

**4–6 hours · Focus:** Understand agents as a loop with tools and stopping conditions—not as magic autonomy.

- [ ] **Complete Unit 1 of Hugging Face's Agents Course.**
  Take the free fundamentals quiz if you want the certificate. No pressure to do the full challenge yet.
  Resource: [Hugging Face AI Agents Course](https://huggingface.co/learn/agents-course) (3–4 hours per unit; free certificate)
- [ ] **Draw the agent loop: goal, tools, observations, and stop condition.**
  Add a failure mode to each box. Where could it loop, use the wrong tool, or trust a bad observation?
- [ ] **Prototype one agent with a safe tool and log every step.**
  Use a calculator or a read-only file lookup first. No write actions without explicit confirmation.

**If you have extra time:** Use Microsoft's AI Agents for Beginners as a second perspective on patterns and RAG.

**Keep this:** An agent mental model and a small, legible prototype.

### Week 11: Give the agent a safe tool

**4–6 hours · Focus:** Learn MCP, then design for least privilege before adding capability.

- [ ] **Complete Unit 1 of Hugging Face's MCP Course.**
  The free fundamentals quiz is the natural checkpoint.
  Resource: [Hugging Face MCP Course](https://huggingface.co/learn/mcp-course) (3–4 hours per unit; free certificate)
- [ ] **Read the opening sections of the official MCP docs and write three sentences about servers, clients, and tools.**
  Use your own words. Jargon you cannot explain is jargon you cannot debug.
  Resource: [Model Context Protocol Documentation](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/) (Reference)
- [ ] **Design a read-only tool and write a safety checklist.**
  List its permissions, data source, what it cannot access, when a human must approve, and where secrets live.

**If you have extra time:** Follow a Microsoft MCP for Beginners lab or complete the rest of the Hugging Face MCP course.

**Keep this:** A clear MCP mental model and a safety checklist for tool use.

### Week 12: Ship, document, review

**5–7 hours · Focus:** Finish one small thing properly and decide what comes next.

- [ ] **Scope the capstone in one paragraph: user, problem, demo, and non-goal.**
  Small and tested beats ambitious and unfinished. Choose one narrow task.
- [ ] **Build and test from a clean setup.**
  Remove personal data and secrets. If a fresh run needs undocumented steps, fix the README or remove the step.
- [ ] **Write the README and share a short case study.**
  Include the problem, audience, how it works, privacy, what works, limitations, and one next step.
- [ ] **Do a 90-day review: list three skills you can demonstrate and two next steps.**
  No self-grade or productivity shame. Evidence and a direction are enough.

**If you have extra time:** Choose one next specialization: evaluation, safety, fine-tuning, local AI, or product craft.

**Keep this:** Capstone project, documentation, and a clear next direction.

### Day 90 checkpoint: you made a thing

The finish line is not a certificate. It is a working artifact, a readable explanation, and a realistic next step.

- [ ] A tested tool, notebook, or manual with a README anyone can follow.
- [ ] One prompt library or workflow you actually reuse.
- [ ] A personal privacy and safety card for AI use.
- [ ] A short case study that names limitations instead of hiding them.
- [ ] A decision about the next 90 days—not a vague intention to 'learn more'.

## When the plan meets a hard week

### You missed a week

Restart at the current week. Do not restart from Week 1. Skip the stretch task and keep the next checkpoint small.

### You only have 15 minutes

Open the next task, set a timer, and do one small piece. Write one sentence in your learning note before you close it.

### A course format is not working

Substitute a video or a different route from the library, but keep the week's artifact. The artifact is the real test.

### Life is genuinely on fire

Pause the plan. Keep your notes. When you return, resume at the current week and shrink the next checkpoint. Do not punish yourself with a restart.

### Perfectionism shows up

Ship the boring first version. A small, documented thing that works teaches more than a beautiful plan that never runs.

## Three projects worth making

### For authors and small businesses

Build a prompt library for book descriptions, FAQ drafts, and email replies—using only public information. Add a human review step and a fact-check checklist.

### For healthcare workers, education only

Practice with fabricated scenarios. Create a study guide or shift-reflection template, never a diagnostic or treatment tool, and never paste real patient information into a consumer AI tool.

### For local-first tinkerers

Compare a hosted model with a small local model on one low-stakes task. Record privacy, cost, speed, and quality trade-offs in a one-page note.

## Day 90: the finish line

- One tested artifact with a README and a clear limitation.
- One reusable prompt or workflow, stored somewhere you will find it again.
- One personal safety and privacy card.
- One honest explanation of what a certificate can and cannot prove.
- One concrete next step for the following 90 days.

The finish line is a thing you made, a clear write-up, and a realistic next step—not a certificate alone.
